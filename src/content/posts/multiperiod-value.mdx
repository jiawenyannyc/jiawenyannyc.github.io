Every practitioner treatment of portfolio construction eventually gets to the
multi-period argument: today's trade changes tomorrow's starting position, so
optimizing each day in isolation must be wrong. It is a correct argument, and it
is usually where the discussion stops — with dynamic programming established as
*better* but never as *better by how much*. That gap matters, because the
multi-period machinery is not free. It needs a model of how alpha decays, and it
buys its optimality by trusting that model.

So I wanted a number. Not "DP dominates" but "DP is worth X% of the objective,
and here is the region of parameter space where X is large enough to care."

**The first thing to establish is that a backtest cannot answer this.** In the
lab below, the cost-aware single-period policy runs at an annualized net Sharpe
of 1.84 and the full dynamic-programming optimum at 1.92 — a real gap, and one
that shows up in the certainty-equivalent as a clean 8%. Simulating 400
independent five-year paths, the standard deviation of a *single* path's Sharpe
is 0.43. To resolve a difference of 0.079 at two standard errors on independent
data, you would need roughly **600 years** of it. Even running both policies on
the *same* 400 paths — paired, common random numbers, the most favorable
comparison available — the DP loses to the myopic policy on 25% of five-year
paths.

That is the whole methodological problem in one line. The effect is real, it is
economically meaningful, and it is invisible at the sample sizes anyone actually
has. Which means it has to be established somewhere the truth is known.

## 1. Building a lab where the answer is computable

The requirement is a model rich enough to contain the multi-period effect and
poor enough that its optimum can be written down. Multi-period value comes from
*coupling* between periods, and coupling needs two ingredients: trading has to
cost something (otherwise each period is independent), and the opportunity has to
change in a predictable way (otherwise there is nothing to look ahead to). The
minimal setting with both is the Gârleanu–Pedersen structure:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{t+1} &= (I - \Phi)\, f_t + \psi_t, & \psi_t &\sim N(0,\, S_\psi) \\
r_{t+1} &= B f_t + u_{t+1}, & u_t &\sim N(0,\, \Sigma) \\
\mathrm{TC}(\Delta x_t) &= \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\, \Delta x_t^{\top} \Sigma\, \Delta x_t
\end{aligned}
$$

with the objective

$$
\max \; \mathbb{E} \sum_t \left[\, x_t^{\top} B f_t
\;-\; \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\, x_t^{\top}\Sigma x_t
\;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\, \Delta x_t^{\top}\Sigma\, \Delta x_t \,\right].
$$

Alpha is driven by mean-reverting factors $f$ (I use two: one with a 5-day
half-life, one with 30), returns load on them through $B$, and trading is charged
quadratically.

Three properties make this worth the loss of realism, and they are the reason to
accept quadratic costs rather than something more faithful:

1. **The optimum is computable.** The problem is linear-quadratic, so the value
   function is quadratic and value iteration on the Bellman equation converges to
   the exact optimal policy — no closed-form formula taken on trust.
2. **Every policy is linear**, of the form $x_t = P x_{t-1} + Q f_t$. Markowitz,
   the cost-aware myopic rule, $T$-step MPC and the DP optimum all live in the
   same family and differ only in $(P, Q)$, so comparisons are apples to apples.
3. **Objective values are exact.** For a linear policy, the state $(x_{t-1},
   f_t)$ is a linear Gaussian system; its stationary covariance solves a discrete
   Lyapunov equation, and every term of the objective reads off that covariance.
   The certainty-equivalent of a policy is computed, not estimated. **No
   Monte-Carlo noise anywhere in the main results.**

The calibration is deliberately mid-range rather than tuned: 15 assets at 24%
annualized volatility, per-asset alpha volatility 3% of asset volatility, risk
aversion $\gamma = 10$, cost scale $\lambda/\gamma = 5$. Every headline number
gets swept over the two parameters that could plausibly change the answer — the
cost scale and the fast factor's half-life — in §5, so nothing rests on the
central point.

## 2. Making sure the solver is right

A "proof by computation" is only as good as the code, and value iteration is easy
to get subtly wrong. The check I trust here is not a unit test but a
**convergence identity between two independent solution methods**.

Model predictive control solves a $T$-step deterministic look-ahead problem,
executes only the first step, and re-solves next period. It has nothing to do
with the Bellman recursion — it is a finite quadratic program in the stacked
future positions. But in a linear-quadratic problem certainty equivalence holds,
so the deterministic look-ahead must converge to the stochastic optimum as $T$
grows. And at $T = 1$, MPC is by construction the single-period cost-aware
problem. So two facts have to hold simultaneously:

| Look-ahead $T$ | max deviation of $P$ from DP | max deviation of $Q$ from DP | equals myopic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.9e−1 | 6.0e−2 | **yes (exactly)** |
| 5 | 6.9e−3 | 2.8e−3 | no |
| 20 | 1.2e−8 | 1.1e−6 | no |
| 40 | **2.2e−16** | **1.1e−16** | no |

Two solvers written from different principles agree to machine precision, and the
$T=1$ case reproduces the myopic policy exactly. That is the validation of both,
and it also means the myopic policy is not a strawman — it is a *member* of the
same MPC family, the $T=1$ member.

As a third check, Monte-Carlo simulation converges to the Lyapunov values as the
simulated horizon grows: at 80 years per path the MC estimate of the DP's
certainty-equivalent is 0.2007 against the exact 0.1985, and the *difference*
between DP and myopic comes in at 0.0165 versus the exact 0.0168.

## 3. What the DP is actually doing differently

Before any numbers, the mechanism — this is the part that survives when the LQ
assumptions do not.

Solve the myopic problem and the optimal policy is
$P = \tfrac{\lambda}{\gamma+\lambda} I$: each period, trade a fixed fraction
$1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{\gamma+\lambda}$ of the way from where you are to the
**cost-free Markowitz target**. At the central calibration that fraction is
$1/6$ — 17% of the gap per day.

Solve the DP and the policy has the *same shape*, $P = c\,I$ with $c$ from the
converged Riccati recursion, but two things change. First, $c = 0.642$, so the DP
closes **36% of the gap per day** — more than twice as fast. Second, and this is
the substantive difference, it is not aiming at the Markowitz target. Writing
the DP's destination as $\text{aim} = (I - P)^{-1} Q f$, the weight it places on
a factor with mean-reversion rate $\phi$ is the factor's Markowitz weight
shrunk by

$$
\theta(\phi) \;=\; \frac{1 - c}{1 - c\,(1 - \phi)}
$$

which is exactly the algebra of an exponentially-weighted average of the current
and all *expected future* Markowitz targets, discounted at the policy's own
persistence $c$. I checked this against the numerically solved policy rather than
asserting it: the formula reproduces the DP's aim to **6e−11** for the fast
factor and **8e−10** for the slow one.

![Left: the weight the DP's aim gives each alpha factor relative to the Markowitz target, as a function of factor half-life, for three cost levels — fast factors are discounted hard, and harder when trading is expensive. Right: the share of the gap to the destination closed per day by the DP and by the myopic policy, versus the transaction-cost scale.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/aim_mechanism.png)

At the central calibration the fast factor (5-day half-life) enters the DP's aim
at **0.81** of its Markowitz weight and the slow factor (30-day) at **0.96**. The
DP is muting the signal that will have decayed before the position is fully on,
and keeping the one that will still be there. Nothing in the myopic problem can
express this — it discounts *nothing*, because it does not know there is a
tomorrow.

Two things worth flagging, because both cut against the usual intuition:

- **The multi-period policy trades more, not less.** In simulation the DP runs
  46× annual turnover against the myopic policy's 31×, and pays 2.2× the
  transaction costs. The folklore that look-ahead makes you patient is backwards
  here. Look-ahead lets you be *aggressive about the right things* — because the
  destination is already discounted for decay, moving toward it fast is safe.
- **This is the same $a/(a+\lambda)$ discount** I bolted onto the signal weights
  in the [signal-combination study](https://github.com/jiawenyannyc/portfolio-construction/tree/main/studies/signal_combination)
  as a heuristic. Here it drops out of the optimality conditions. That is the
  useful transfer: you can get most of the multi-period benefit by discounting
  fast signals in a single-period problem, without any dynamic program at all.

![Positions in one asset over 150 simulated days under three policies. The cost-blind Markowitz book jumps onto the target and jitters; the cost-aware myopic book lags it smoothly; the DP book tracks closer, having already discounted the fast component of the target.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/policy_illustration.png)

## 4. The ladder — and the rung that actually matters

Now the numbers. Exact certainty-equivalents, annualized, decomposed into their
three terms:

| Policy | Alpha | − Risk | − Cost | **CE** | % of DP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Markowitz monthly (fixed frequency) | | | | −0.065 | — |
| Markowitz weekly (fixed frequency) | | | | −0.002 | — |
| Markowitz daily (cost-blind) | 0.515 | 0.257 | 0.216 | 0.041 | 21% |
| **Single-period cost-aware, repeated** | 0.376 | 0.182 | 0.013 | **0.182** | **91.6%** |
| MPC, 5-step look-ahead | 0.405 | 0.179 | 0.028 | 0.198 | 99.9% |
| **Dynamic programming (optimal)** | 0.397 | 0.171 | 0.027 | **0.199** | 100% |

(The two fixed-frequency rows require Monte-Carlo — a calendar rule is not a
linear policy — so they carry sampling error; everything else is exact.)

![Certainty-equivalent and net Sharpe by policy at the central calibration, with Monte-Carlo error bars and annualized turnover.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/policy_comparison.png)

The headline is the *ordering of the gaps*, not any single number:

- **Cost-blind → cost-aware: 4.4×** (0.041 → 0.182). The cost-blind book earns
  the most alpha of anything in the table, 0.515, and gives 0.216 of it straight
  back in trading. Putting one penalty term in the one-period objective recovers
  almost all of that.
- **Cost-aware → optimal: +9%** (0.182 → 0.199). Real, and one-tenth the size of
  the first step.
- **Fixed-frequency rebalancing is dominated** by even the cost-blind daily
  policy, and monthly rebalancing has a *negative* certainty-equivalent here.
  Slowing down on the calendar is a crude proxy for a cost penalty: it reduces
  turnover, but it cannot tell an urgent trade from a marginal one, which is
  precisely what the penalty does.

Read the decomposition row by row and the DP's edge is visible in the mechanism
of §3: relative to myopic it buys 0.021 more alpha and 0.011 less risk, and pays
0.015 more in costs for the privilege.

## 5. When does it matter? The map

The 8% gap at the central calibration is one point in a parameter space, and the
two parameters that should govern it are the ones that create the coupling: how
expensive trading is, and how fast alpha decays. Sweeping both and evaluating
exactly at every grid point:

![Heatmap of the certainty-equivalent lost by the repeated single-period policy relative to DP, over transaction-cost scale (columns) and fast-signal half-life (rows). Losses run from under 1% at cheap trading to 46% at extreme costs.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/myopia_loss_heatmap.png)

- **Cheap trading (λ/γ ≤ 0.5): under 1%.** Myopia is essentially free. This is
  the formal version of the obvious point — with no friction, the periods do not
  couple, and there is nothing for look-ahead to exploit.
- **Expensive trading (λ/γ ≥ 20): 20–46%.** When every trade is precious,
  knowing where you will want to be next month is most of the problem.
- **Cost drives it; speed only modulates it.** Moving across the cost axis at
  fixed speed changes the loss by an order of magnitude; moving across the speed
  axis at fixed cost changes it by a factor of two. And the speed effect is
  **hump-shaped**, not monotone — at λ/γ = 50 the loss peaks at a 8-day half-life
  (46%) and falls off in both directions. Ultra-fast alpha is hopeless for
  everyone and both policies decline to chase it; slow alpha is easy for
  everyone. The dangerous zone is alpha *just* fast enough to tempt a myopic
  optimizer into buying something that will be gone before the position is on.
- **At realistic frictions (λ/γ ≈ 2–5, half-life 3–5 days): 2.5–9%.**

Worth noting what the same map says about the *other* comparison: at the central
calibration, cost-blindness costs 79% of the DP's certainty-equivalent, and in
the expensive corner it goes past 100% — the cost-blind policy's CE turns
negative. The two ladder steps are not close.

## 6. How much look-ahead do you actually need?

If the DP is worth 9% at the central calibration, the practical question is
whether you need the Bellman equation to collect it. MPC with horizon $T$ is a
small convex quadratic program — something you can put in production with
constraints, which the DP cannot handle. So: exact CE as a function of $T$.

![Percentage of the DP certainty-equivalent captured by MPC as a function of look-ahead horizon, rising from 91.6% at T=1 to essentially 100% by T=5.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/mpc_horizon.png)

| Look-ahead $T$ | % of DP captured |
|---|---|
| 1 (= repeated single-period) | 91.6% |
| 2 | 98.4% |
| 3 | 99.5% |
| 5 | 99.9% |

**Two steps of look-ahead close 80% of the myopia gap; five close all of it.**
The value of look-ahead is concentrated almost entirely in the first step or two
— which makes sense given §3, where the aim is an exponentially-weighted average
whose weights decay at $c = 0.64$ per period.

This is the actionable result. Nobody needs an infinite-horizon dynamic program
in production; a two-to-five step convex QP is indistinguishable from optimal,
and unlike the DP it survives contact with constraints, transaction-cost models
that are not quadratic, and everything else real books have. It is also why
Boyd et al.'s multi-period convex framework is the industry standard rather than
Riccati recursions.

## 7. Real data, honestly

The simulation says look-ahead should be worth roughly 10% of a small number.
Running it on an actual panel is not going to *prove* anything — see the 600-year
estimate at the top — but it is worth checking the sign and the mechanism on the
5-signal composite from the [signal-combination study](https://github.com/jiawenyannyc/portfolio-construction/tree/main/studies/signal_combination):
200 S&P 500 names, 2011–2024, daily cost-penalised QP with dollar-neutrality,
GMV and per-name position limits, spread plus impact costs.

The myopic engine ($T = 1$) is compared against a blocked-MPC version ($T = 3$,
each block about one alpha horizon, forecasts decayed along the horizon at the
composite's fitted rate of 0.060/day) — identical data, costs, signals and
constraints, differing only in look-ahead.

![Left: net Sharpe against annualized turnover for the myopic and look-ahead engines at three cost-aversion levels. Right: net Sharpe by cost aversion, with and without look-ahead.](@/assets/research/multiperiod-value/real_data_lookahead.png)

| Cost aversion $\kappa$ | gross Sharpe $T{=}1$ → $T{=}3$ | net Sharpe $T{=}1$ → $T{=}3$ | turnover $T{=}1$ → $T{=}3$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.29 → 0.32 | −0.044 → −0.034 | 15.9× → 13.2× |
| 5 | 0.29 → 0.32 | 0.130 → **0.114** | 6.7× → 5.2× |
| 10 | 0.22 → 0.28 | 0.145 → **0.168** | 2.3× → 1.9× |

What is consistent: look-ahead raises gross Sharpe at every cost-aversion level
and cuts turnover 17–23% at every level. That is the mechanism from §3 showing up
in the right place — the engine declines to chase the fast component it knows
will decay.

What does not follow the script: **net Sharpe gets worse at κ = 5**, 0.130 →
0.114, even as gross rises and turnover falls. Only at κ = 10 does the
improvement land on the bottom line (0.145 → 0.168). Three points on one
historical path, with a single-path Sharpe standard error of roughly 0.27 over
14 years, is not evidence of anything at this effect size — including not
evidence *against*. It is also worth being explicit that the blocked-MPC
approximation is coarse: three blocks of ~21 days with exponentially decayed
alphas is a much cruder look-ahead than the lab's daily steps, and the real
engine's L1 spread term creates a no-trade region the LQ analysis does not have.

I would rather report this as it came out than pick the κ that agrees with the
simulation. The general lesson is the one the whole study is built around: the
value of multi-period optimization is established in simulation, where the truth
is known, and real data is where you check that nothing about the implementation
is broken.

## 8. What I would actually do

1. **Get a cost term into your single-period objective.** That is the 4×. If you
   are rebalancing on a calendar to control turnover, this is a strictly better
   instrument, and the study shows fixed-frequency rules being beaten by every
   cost-aware policy in the table.
2. **Discount fast signals in the target itself.** The $\theta(\phi)$ shrinkage
   of §3 is a one-line change to how a composite is built, and captures a good
   part of what the DP knows with no dynamic program and no extra machinery.
3. **Add two to five steps of look-ahead if trading is expensive relative to
   alpha.** In the cheap-trading half of the map there is under 1% in it and the
   added model risk is not worth it. In the expensive corner it is 20–46% and the
   argument reverses completely.
4. **Do not build the infinite-horizon DP.** It captures ~0.1% more than MPC(5)
   and cannot take a constraint.

## 9. Honest limitations

1. **The lab's cleanliness is bought with LQ assumptions.** Quadratic costs (no
   bid-ask spread, so no no-trade region), no constraints, Gaussian everything.
   Each departure moves the numbers; I do not expect any of them to move the
   ordering, but the quadratic-cost assumption is the one doing the most work —
   an L1 spread term generates a no-trade region that captures some of the same
   benefit the look-ahead is being credited with here.
2. **Known dynamics is the big one.** The DP is handed the true $\Phi$ and $B$.
   Estimated decay rates hit the look-ahead policy hardest, because its entire
   edge is a bet on the decay model; the myopic policy has less to be wrong
   about. The gaps here are an **upper bound** on what multi-period optimization
   is worth in practice, and a 9% upper bound is a useful thing to know before
   building the machinery.
3. **Two factors, one speed each.** Real books have a spectrum of decay rates and
   time-varying ones. The hump shape in §5 suggests where the interesting
   interactions would be.
4. **The real-data section is one path.** Stated above, restated here: it
   illustrates, it does not test.
5. **Nothing here says anything about the cases where multi-period optimization
   is structural rather than marginal** — hard path-dependent constraints,
   predictable flows, liquidation schedules, tax lots. In this study the periods
   couple only through costs and alpha decay, which is the *weakest* form of
   coupling there is. The right reading of a 9% result is "coupling through costs
   alone is worth single digits," not "multi-period optimization is worth single
   digits."
