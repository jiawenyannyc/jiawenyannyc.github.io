**Goal.** Put a number on the gap between a backtested Sharpe ratio and the
evidence it actually represents. This post covers the statistical machinery for
that: where selection bias comes from, how to measure the **probability of
backtest overfitting (PBO)**, and how the **probabilistic** and **deflated**
Sharpe ratios turn a point estimate into a probability statement that accounts
for how hard you searched.

Everything below is simulation. There is no strategy here to be right or wrong
about — the point is the measurement apparatus, which is the part that
generalizes. Part 2 will take the same machinery to synthetic data: calibrated
generators, deep generative models, and what it means to validate a rule on paths
that never happened.

## 1. Most backtest flaws are procedural. One isn't.

The standard catalogue of backtesting sins is well known: survivorship bias,
look-ahead bias, storytelling, ignored transaction costs, an edge concentrated in
a handful of outliers, assuming a costless short. Every one of these is a
**process defect**, and every one is fixable by discipline — record point-in-time
release dates, include delisted names, charge realistic spread and impact, check
what happens when you drop the top 1% of days.

**Selection bias is different in kind.** It survives a perfectly clean pipeline.
Run enough configurations over data with no exploitable structure at all and the
best one will look skilled — not because anything leaked, but because you took a
maximum. Reporting only the winner is the entire mechanism. You cannot tidy your
way out of it; it has to be corrected *statistically*, by charging the result for
the size of the search that produced it.

Which means the single most important number about a backtest is one that does
not appear in the backtest: **how many things you tried.**

## 2. How much Sharpe does a search manufacture?

Take $N$ strategies, all with a true Sharpe of exactly zero, each backtested over
$T$ observations. Each estimated Sharpe $\widehat{SR}_n$ is noise centred on zero
with standard deviation $\approx 1/\sqrt{T}$. The *maximum* over $N$ such
estimates is not centred on zero — it grows roughly like

$$
\mathrm{E}\!\left[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\right] \;\approx\; \sqrt{V}\,\sqrt{2\ln N},
\qquad V = \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}\right]
$$

The $\sqrt{2\ln N}$ form is the textbook asymptotic, and it is noticeably
optimistic at the trial counts people actually run. Bailey and López de Prado's
Gumbel approximation is the one to use:

$$
\mathrm{E}\!\left[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\right] \;\approx\;
\sqrt{V}\left[(1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\right)
\;+\; \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N e}\right)\right]
$$

with $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Simulating it
directly — 3,000 replications of $N$ independent zero-edge backtests, each 1,260
daily observations — the Gumbel form is essentially exact while $\sqrt{2\ln N}$
overshoots by ~15% at $N = 1{,}000$ (3.26 vs 3.72 against a Monte-Carlo 3.25).

![Left: expected maximum Sharpe under a zero-skill null versus the number of trials, comparing Monte Carlo against the sqrt(2 ln N) asymptotic and the Gumbel approximation. Right: the same curve translated into annualized Sharpe for a five-year daily backtest.](@/assets/research/backtest-overfitting-part-1/selection_bias.png)

The right panel is the one worth internalizing. Translated into annualized Sharpe
for a **5-year daily backtest**, where no strategy in the search has any edge
whatsoever:

| Trials $N$ | Expected best annualized Sharpe |
|---|---|
| 4 | 0.50 |
| 45 | **1.00** |
| 100 | 1.13 |
| 1,000 | **1.46** |

Forty-five configurations — one afternoon of grid search over two parameters — is
enough to produce a Sharpe of 1.0 out of pure noise. A thousand trials, which is
a modest hyperparameter sweep, gets you to 1.46. Neither number is a finding.
Both are the expected output of the search.

## 3. Measuring the damage: PBO

Knowing that selection bias inflates the winner is not the same as knowing
whether *your* selection was inflated. The **probability of backtest overfitting**
answers a sharper question:

> When I pick the in-sample-best configuration, how often does it land **below the
> median** out of sample?

If in-sample rank carries real information, the in-sample winner should usually
stay near the top out of sample and PBO is small. If it carries none, the winner
is a coin flip against its peers and PBO approaches 50%. Above 50% and in-sample
rank is actively *misleading* — the selection is picking the configuration that
was luckiest, and luck reverses.

**Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV)** estimates it without
committing to a single train/test split. Build a $T \times N$ matrix of
per-period returns, one column per configuration. Cut the rows into $S$ contiguous
blocks; for every one of the $\binom{S}{S/2}$ ways to choose half the blocks as
in-sample, take the complement as out-of-sample, find the in-sample winner
$n^{*}$, and record its **relative rank** $\omega_c \in (0,1)$ among the
out-of-sample Sharpes. The logit

$$
\lambda_c = \log\frac{\omega_c}{1 - \omega_c}, \qquad
\mathrm{PBO} = \Pr[\lambda < 0]
$$

is negative exactly when the winner underperforms the out-of-sample median, so
PBO is the mass of the $\lambda$ distribution to the left of zero. The symmetry
matters: each block serves as training and test data equally often, so there is
no privileged split to tune against.

Running it on two synthetic families of 50 configurations each — $S = 10$ blocks,
252 splits, 1,260 daily observations, with a common factor so the configurations
correlate the way variants of one strategy do:

![Left: CSCV logit distributions for a skilled and an overfit strategy family, with the mass below zero marking PBO. Right: for the overfit family, out-of-sample Sharpe of the in-sample winner plotted against its in-sample Sharpe, with a negative fitted slope.](@/assets/research/backtest-overfitting-part-1/pbo.png)

| Family | PBO | Median OOS Sharpe of the IS winner |
|---|---|---|
| Skilled (one configuration has a genuine edge) | **3%** | strongly positive |
| Overfit (no configuration has any edge) | **62%** | −0.02 |

The skilled family piles up at high $\lambda$: the true best configuration is
found in nearly every split. The overfit family spreads across the whole range
with 62% of its mass below zero — worse than a coin flip, because when
configurations are correlated the in-sample winner is disproportionately the one
with the largest *idiosyncratic* luck, and that reverses out of sample.

The right panel makes the same point without any ranks. Regressing the winner's
out-of-sample Sharpe on its in-sample Sharpe gives a slope of **−0.61**. Not
attenuated toward zero — *negative*. In an overfit family, a better in-sample
number predicts a worse out-of-sample one. That is the shape of the thing every
walk-forward chart is designed to hide behind a single path.

## 4. Is this Sharpe distinguishable from zero? The PSR

Selection bias is one problem; estimation error is another, and the Sharpe ratio
is a badly behaved estimator. Its sampling distribution depends on the third and
fourth moments of returns, and strategies that sell tail risk have exactly the
skew and kurtosis that make a naive $t$-test optimistic.

The **probabilistic Sharpe ratio** fixes both by reporting a probability instead
of a point estimate:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{PSR}}(SR^{*}) = \Phi\!\left[
\frac{\left(\widehat{SR} - SR^{*}\right)\sqrt{T-1}}
{\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^{\,2}}}
\right]
$$

where $\gamma_3$ is skewness, $\gamma_4$ raw kurtosis, and $SR^{*}$ the benchmark
being tested against. The denominator is the whole story: **negative skew
increases the standard error** (the $-\gamma_3\widehat{SR}$ term grows), and so
does excess kurtosis. Inverting for the length of record needed to clear a
confidence level $\alpha$ gives the **minimum track record length**:

$$
\mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + \left[1 - \gamma_3\widehat{SR}
+ \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^{\,2}\right]
\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}{\widehat{SR} - SR^{*}}\right)^{2}
$$

![Left: PSR against track record length for an observed annualized Sharpe of 1.0 under three return distributions. Right: minimum track record length in months needed for 95% confidence, as a function of the observed Sharpe.](@/assets/research/backtest-overfitting-part-1/psr.png)

For an observed annualized Sharpe of 1.0 on monthly returns, the months required
to be 95% confident the true Sharpe is positive:

| Return distribution | MinTRL |
|---|---|
| Normal ($\gamma_3 = 0,\ \gamma_4 = 3$) | 35 months |
| Fat tails ($\gamma_3 = 0,\ \gamma_4 = 8$) | 38 months |
| Negative skew + fat tails ($\gamma_3 = -1.5,\ \gamma_4 = 8$) | **52 months** |

Same Sharpe, same confidence level, **50% more data required** once the return
distribution looks like an actual short-volatility book. A note on units, because
it changes the reading: these higher-moment terms scale with $\widehat{SR}^{\,2}$
in per-observation units, so at daily frequency ($\widehat{SR} \approx 0.06$)
they are numerically invisible and PSR collapses to the plain $t$-statistic. The
correction bites at monthly frequency — which is also how allocators actually see
a Sharpe ratio reported.

## 5. Deflating for the search: the DSR

PSR tests one track record in isolation. The **deflated Sharpe ratio** is PSR
with the benchmark set to what the search alone would have produced:

$$
\mathrm{DSR} = \widehat{\mathrm{PSR}}\left(SR^{*}\right), \qquad
SR^{*} = \sqrt{V}\left[(1-\gamma)\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\right)
+ \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N e}\right)\right]
$$

That $SR^{*}$ is exactly the expected maximum from Section 2. The test is no
longer "is this Sharpe greater than zero" but **"is this Sharpe greater than the
best number my search was always going to find."**

![Left: the deflated Sharpe threshold rising with the number of trials, against a fixed observed Sharpe of 1.5. Right: the DSR of that strategy collapsing as the trial count grows.](@/assets/research/backtest-overfitting-part-1/dsr.png)

For a selected strategy showing an annualized Sharpe of **1.5** over a 5-year
daily backtest:

| Effective trials $N$ | Threshold $SR^{*}$ | DSR |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.70 | 0.96 |
| 100 | 1.13 | 0.79 |
| 1,000 | 1.46 | **0.54** |

The observed number never changes. The evidence it constitutes collapses — from
significant at 95%, to inconclusive, to a coin flip. The strategy is significant
at the 95% level only if the search behind it was **13 trials or fewer**; by
~1,400 trials the threshold has caught up with the observed Sharpe entirely and
DSR hits 0.50.

**Is a DSR of 0.54 a rejection?** No — and treating it as one is its own mistake.
It is a statement that the backtest carries almost no information either way. The
correct responses are to shrink the search, extend the sample, or hold the
strategy in paper for a defined period and re-test — not to bin it, and
emphatically not to run more variants until one clears the bar, which raises $N$
and moves the bar with it.

## 6. What this changes in practice

The machinery only works if you can state $N$, which turns a statistical result
into an operational requirement: **keep a trials ledger.** Every configuration
evaluated against the data goes in it, not just the ones retained. A search whose
size is unknown cannot be deflated, and an undeflatable backtest is an
uninterpretable one.

Three practical notes on doing that honestly:

1. **Trials are not independent, so raw $N$ overstates the search.** Fifty
   variants of one momentum signal are nearly the same trial; fifty unrelated
   signal families are not. What the formula wants is an *effective* trial count.
   Clustering the trial returns and counting clusters is the standard
   approximation, and it is a genuinely soft number — quote DSR as a range across
   a plausible $N$, not a point.
2. **The count starts before the code does.** Configurations you rejected by
   eyeballing a chart, features you dropped after a first look, the universe you
   switched to when the first one disappointed — all of it is selection, and none
   of it appears in a parameter grid.
3. **The threshold rises with $\sqrt{V}$ too.** A search whose trial Sharpes are
   widely dispersed produces a larger maximum than one where they cluster. $V$ is
   estimated from the trial set, which is another reason to keep the losers.

## 7. Honest limitations

1. **The nulls here are clean.** Zero-edge trials with iid Gaussian returns and,
   in the CSCV experiment, a single common factor. Real strategy families are
   correlated in messier, time-varying ways, which affects both $V$ and the
   effective trial count. The *shape* of these curves is robust; the specific
   trial counts are illustrative.
2. **DSR assumes trial Sharpes are approximately Gaussian across trials.** With
   strongly non-normal trial distributions the Gumbel maximum is an
   approximation, though a good one in the regimes tested here.
3. **PBO is a property of a family, not a verdict on a strategy.** A low PBO says
   your selection procedure has signal; it does not say the selected
   configuration will make money net of costs. Capacity and cost analysis is a
   separate gate — see the [capacity study](/posts/capacity-curves) for what
   happens to a Sharpe-0.52 signal once you charge it for spread.
4. **No multiple-testing correction is a substitute for held-out data.** All of
   this deflates a number computed on data you have already looked at. It raises
   the bar; it does not manufacture new evidence.

## 8. Coming in Part 2

The natural response to "one historical path is not enough evidence" is to
manufacture more paths. Part 2 takes up backtesting on **synthetic data**: mean-
reverting processes with a known edge as a controlled testbed, the taxonomy of
generators (block bootstrap, parametric, deep generative), and where TimeVAE,
TimeGAN and diffusion models actually help. Plus the uncomfortable part — a
generator that memorizes its training set provides no independent evidence, and
optimizing a rule over synthetic paths overfits the *generator* rather than the
market. Selection bias does not go away when you make more data. It just gets
harder to see.
